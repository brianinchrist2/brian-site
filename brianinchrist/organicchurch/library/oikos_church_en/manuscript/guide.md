# Study and Discussion Handbook

## How to Use This Guide
This manual provides structured discussion material for every chapter of the book and is suited to the following three settings:
- Small-group reading sessions: 5–12 people, guided by a facilitator, covering 1–2 chapters per gathering. It is recommended to select themes by topic, with each theme taking about 15–20 minutes. When time is limited, you may choose only one theme.
- In-house study within the house church: use it alongside fellowship meals or interactive gatherings. You can work backward from the "Practice Challenge" and expand on the theme most relevant to the week's life.
- Personal study: after reading a chapter, first use the Guiding Questions to test whether you have grasped the core argument, then use the Exploratory Questions for deeper reflection.
About the question types:
- Guiding Questions — help you review and understand the chapter's core arguments. The answers can basically be found in the book; the aim is to ensure that the material has been "truly understood" and to build a shared foundation for subsequent discussion.
- Exploratory Questions — there are no standard answers. They invite participants to compare the arguments with their own experience, reason through the consequences of different choices, and respond to possible objections, forming their own judgments.
Note for facilitators: each theme contains 4 questions (2 Guiding + 2 Exploratory). If time allows, cover them all; if time is limited, it is recommended to discuss at least 1 Guiding Question and 1 Exploratory Question per theme. The "This Week's Practice Challenge" at the end of each chapter works well as a shared commitment before closing the gathering.

## Preface: The Contextual Call of the Chinese Church
### Chapter Overview
The preface sets out the theological stance and contextual starting point of this book's writing: the author holds "Sola Scriptura" as his core conviction, insisting that Scripture settles not only soteriology but also the form and governance of the church. The author observes that the contemporary Chinese church has fallen into a "paradigm disarray" — its body has been sent home by circumstances, yet its theology still lingers in the great hall. The root of this predicament is the long-standing absence of a biblically grounded ontological system for the house church. The preface also transparently explains the book's four hermeneutical principles: distinguishing descriptive from normative statements, recognizing that sovereign use does not equal design approval, positioning Barna's data as merely auxiliary, and critiquing structures rather than pastors. In the end, the book's critique is a summons — calling brothers and sisters to return together to "the pattern shown on the mountain."
### Key Scriptures
- Exodus 25:40 — "Be careful to make everything according to the pattern shown you on the mountain" (the central image of the book)
- Numbers 22:28 — God speaks to Balaam through the donkey (an example that "sovereign use does not equal design approval")
### Theme One: Is the Form of the Church a "Biblical Norm" or a "Cultural Choice"?
Guiding Questions
1.
The preface argues that the principle of "Sola Scriptura" must be carried through to ecclesiology, not limited only to soteriology. Why does the author insist on this? What consequences does he believe will follow from "treating the form of the church as a cultural choice"?
2.
The first hermeneutical principle in the preface distinguishes "descriptive statements" from "normative claims." Explain in your own words the difference between the two, and describe which practices of the early church the author considers to have normative significance that transcends the ages.
Exploratory Questions
1.
(Experience comparison) In the faith tradition in which you grew up, were the forms of church gatherings (style of the pulpit, frequency of communion, leadership structure, etc.) generally regarded as "simply the way things are" or as "open to discussion"? How has this default stance shaped the church culture you have personally experienced?
2.
(Position response) Some people say, "As long as God is in the heart, the form doesn't matter; the outward structure of the church is unimportant." Based on the argument of the preface, evaluate the adequacy of this response — how would the author rebut it, and how would you yourself judge it?
### Theme Two: Where Does the Crisis of the Chinese Church Lie?
Guiding Questions
1.
The preface describes how, after the Chinese urban church was forced to scatter under environmental pressure, what occurred was "technical maintenance" rather than genuine transformation. Explain the difference between the two: what does "technical maintenance" mean? What does the author believe is missing?
2.
The author says the contemporary Chinese church lacks an "ontological foundation," with the result that the practice of the house church can only remain at the level of "technique and methods." List the specific consequences of this lack that the preface mentions.
Exploratory Questions
1.
(Scenario reasoning) Suppose a church is forced to shift to small-group gatherings under external pressure, but the pastor still preaches weekly through livestreaming and the congregation still gives through online transfer. Reason through: after a year, what changes might occur in this church's spiritual state? What problems would surface, and what problems would be covered up?
2.
(Consequence analysis) The preface distinguishes "critique of structure" from "critique of pastors." Do you think this distinction is easy to maintain in practice? If believers in a church read this book but turn their criticism toward the individual pastor, what might happen? How can such a misreading be avoided?

## Introduction: From the "Dilemma of Form" to the "Call of the Kingdom"
### Chapter Overview
The Introduction lifts the book's central question from the level of circumstance to the level of theology: the crisis of the contemporary church is, in essence, a "crisis of obedience"—not a lack of better ministry strategies, but a departure from the pattern of the church revealed in the New Testament. The Introduction sets out the "sacred triangle" framework—Oikos (house / the sacred vessel), Koinonia (life-sharing fellowship / the sacred ecology), and Oikonomia (household governance / the sacred mechanism)—which together answer the three most fundamental questions: "What is the church, how does it live, and how does it operate?" At the macro level, the Introduction embeds ecclesiology within the narrative of the Kingdom: the church does not exist for its own sake, but for the sake of the missionary plan that God is advancing throughout the earth. For the Chinese church, the house church should not be understood as a compromise under external pressure (Plan B), but recognized as active obedience within God's design (Plan A).
### Key Scriptures
• Exodus 25:40 —"the pattern shown on the mountain" (the analogical foundation for the New Testament blueprint)
• Matthew 28:18-20 —the Great Commission (the foundation of the church as a missionary community)
• Acts 2:44-45 —"they had all things in common" (historical confirmation of what authentic Koinonia looks like)
### Theme One: How Does the "Sacred Triangle" Frame the Entire Book?
Guiding Questions
1.
The Introduction presents the three concepts of the "sacred triangle," each answering one most fundamental question. Please match them up: what question does Oikos answer? What question does Koinonia answer? What question does Oikonomia answer? Is the relationship among these three parallel, or is there some intrinsic order of dependence?
2.
The Introduction says that Koinonia goes far beyond "fellowship activities"; it is "a life attitude willing to share one's own resources, time, pain, and joy completely with another member." Please identify at least two points from the Introduction's description that show how authentic Koinonia differs from "small-group gatherings" in the ordinary sense.
Exploratory Questions
1.
(Experiential contrast) Use the "sacred triangle" to examine the church or gathering in which you currently participate: Oikos (a life-community built around the household), Koinonia (deep life-sharing fellowship), Oikonomia (spiritual-parent-style governance)—which corner do you think is the weakest? What is the root of that weakness—insufficient theological understanding, structural barriers, or resistance of the human heart?
2.
(Scenario projection) The Introduction says the sacred triangle is "the holographic projection of the triune God within the church"—Oikos manifests the Father's household, Koinonia manifests the Son's body, and Oikonomia manifests the movement of the Spirit. If a gathering community decided to deliberately practice these three dimensions, imagine concretely: at what moments and in what ways in their daily lives (not only during meeting times) could they see all three dimensions operating at the same time?
### Theme Two: What Does It Mean to Move from "Redemption-Centered" to "Kingdom-Centered"?
Guiding Questions
1.
The Introduction distinguishes two theological perspectives: a view of the church centered on "redemption," and a view of the church centered on the "Kingdom." Based on the Introduction's argument, explain what substantive differences these two perspectives have in their understanding of the "mission of the church."
2.
The Introduction specifically points out that the Chinese church faces not a mere "methodological crisis" but a "crisis of obedience." How do the two differ? Does the author mean that the problem lies in believers' willingness rather than in their ability? Please find evidence from the Introduction to support your answer.
Exploratory Questions
1.
(Consequence analysis) According to the Introduction's argument, if a church sets its own prosperity (growth in numbers, financial stability, abundant activities) as its priority goal rather than "expanding the Kingdom," what specific consequences may appear in the long term? Are these consequences already visible in today's churches?
2.
(Stance response) Someone might say: "The house church is an unavoidable choice forced upon us by the circumstances; once the environment improves, we should still return to the normal pattern of building churches." Based on the argument of the fourth section of the Introduction, evaluate this stance from the framework of "Plan A / Plan B"—what theological assumption lies behind it? Where is the most critical point of divergence?

## Chapter 1: The Loss of Identity
### Chapter Overview
Chapter 1 centers on "structural diagnosis," revealing that the true root of the contemporary church's crisis lies not in external persecution but in a deep "loss of identity"—unawares, we have replaced the spiritual blueprint of the New Testament church with the forms of pagan culture. The chapter takes apart, one by one, the three conceptual misunderstandings that obstruct the return (the spatial myth, the expediency trap, and evolutionary arrogance); traces how "the temple complex" causes a misplacement of focus and degrades believers from disciples into spectators; and, through specific observations of China's urban churches, shows that consumerist spirituality and spiritual loneliness have become the structurally inevitable products of the institutional church. The whole chapter points toward a striking theological verdict: the temple complex, the clerical hierarchy, and consumerist spirituality correspond respectively to the threefold loss of Oikos, Oikonomia, and Koinonia—patchwork reform cannot reach the root of the disease; only a thorough ontological revolution can bring true renewal.
### Key Scriptures
• Acts 2:46 —believers broke bread "in the temple and in homes" (the early church's active practice built around the household)
• Acts 17:24 —"God... does not dwell in temples made with hands" (a theological declaration that radically relativizes all physical buildings)
• Hebrews 10:24-25 —"consider how to stir up one another to love... encourage one another" (the essence of a gathering is multidirectional life interaction, not one-way reception of information)
### Theme One: Three Major Misunderstandings: Our Cognitive Distortions About the Church
Guiding Questions
1.

In your own words, explain one by one what the core error of each of these misunderstandings is, and why the author calls them "confusions at the level of ontology"—mistaking "form" for "substance"?
2.
On the "expediency trap," the author cites two passages of Scripture: Acts 2:46 and 20:20. How do these two passages support the claim that "the house church is an active theological choice"? The author distinguishes "Plan A" from "Plan B"—in one sentence, state the fundamental difference between the two in theological essence.
Exploratory Questions
1.
(Experiential contrast) In the church environments you have personally experienced, which of these three misunderstandings is the most deeply entrenched and the hardest to unsettle? Give a specific scene or conversation as an example, and explain how this misunderstanding operates quietly within everyday faith life.
2.
(Scenario projection) Imagine that the urban church you belong to is forced to scatter due to external pressure, and the small groups begin meeting in members' homes. Three months later, a facilitator says during a discussion: "Once the situation stabilizes, we should still find a place to gather centrally again—that way we will have more influence." Based on this chapter's argument, project what follows: if this mindset continues to dominate, what foreseeable changes may occur in the gathering ecology? Which windows of opportunity will quietly close during those three months?

(Consequence analysis) This chapter points out that when a church makes "maintaining the worship machine" its first priority, genuine disciple multiplication is structurally marginalized—even if the one at the pulpit sincerely proclaims mission. ## Chapter Two: Searching for the Blueprint—"The Pattern Shown on the Mountain" as a Hermeneutical Key
### Chapter Overview
This chapter takes Exodus 25:40—"See that you make them after the pattern shown to you on the mountain"—as the hermeneutical key of the whole book, arguing that the building of the church must be faithful to God's revelation rather than to human invention. The author traces the historical trajectory of "the pattern," which evolves from the material dimension of the Old Testament (the dimensions of stone and brick, the shapes of the vessels) to the dimension of life and relationship in the New Testament (Oikos, Koinonia, Oikonomia), arguing that this evolution is an elevation, not an abandonment. The chapter's core methodological contribution is its proposal of a "threefold test"—the authority of explicit teaching, the signals of repetition and universality, and the overall trajectory of redemptive history—as a verifiable framework for deriving normative claims from biblical descriptions. The chapter also exposes the crux of the "paradigm split" in the modern church: doctrine comes from the New Testament, while structure comes from the Old Testament, even from pagan Greco-Roman culture—using the single-preacher model to show how "supplementing" can in effect "abolish" a biblical command.
### Key Scriptures
• Exodus 25:40 — "See that you make them after the pattern shown to you on the mountain" (the hermeneutical key of the whole book)
• Hebrews 8:5 — the Old Testament sanctuary is "a copy and shadow of the heavenly things" (the basis for the evolution from "shadow to substance")
• 1 Corinthians 14:26 — "When you come together, each one has a hymn or a teaching…" (the explicit teaching of a gathering model in which every member participates)

## Chapter Three: God's Plan and Mission—The Grand Narrative of the One Kingdom
### Chapter Overview
From the standpoint of biblical theology, this chapter overturns the "salvation-centeredness" popular in contemporary evangelicalism, arguing that God has never had two plans: redemption exists to restore the Kingdom plan of creation, not to replace it. Genesis 1:28—"Be fruitful and multiply, fill the earth and subdue it"—is God's most original Kingdom mission, and the Great Commission of Matthew 28:19 is the spiritual upgrade of that same mission. Drawing on the missiologists Newbigin and Bosch, the author argues that mission is not "one thing the church does," but the very essence of the church's existence; and citing the archaeologist Snyder's evidence, he shows that the early church's deliberate choice of households rather than temples for three full centuries before Constantine was itself moving in the direction of God's plan. Revelation 21:22—"I saw no temple in the city"—is the end-times theological declaration: the household is the ultimate form of God's dwelling place, and the temple is nothing more than scaffolding along the way.
### Key Scriptures
• Genesis 1:28 — "Be fruitful and multiply, fill the earth and subdue it" (the creation mandate as the starting point of the Kingdom plan)
• Ephesians 1:10 — "to unite all things in him in the fullness of time, things in heaven and things on earth" (the declaration of the Kingdom's cosmic goal)
• Revelation 21:22 — "I saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb" (the end reveals that the household is the ultimate vessel of God's presence)

## Chapter 4: The Sacred Prototype — Oikos and the Organic Principle of Expansion
### Chapter Overview
This chapter argues from four dimensions that "Oikos is not one of the many options available to the Kingdom of God, but the original unit of Kingdom governance." First, Oikos precedes all religious institutions in time: God's election of Abraham was explicitly Oikos-based (Gen 18:19); Jesus liberated the family from the limits of bloodline and elevated it into a spiritual covenant family that can expand without limit. Second, from the family discipleship prototype of Deuteronomy 6, to Daniel's viability in Babylon, to the intergenerational transmission of Lois and Eunice in the Timothy chain, to Jesus' mobile Oikos of "constantly being with his disciples" — the core mechanism of discipleship has always been shared life, not a curriculum system. Third, the contrast of two expansion logics: "addition accumulation" based at the meeting hall (dependent on scarce resources, unable to reproduce itself) versus "multiplication reproduction" centered on the spiritual family (cell-division-like, independent of scarce resources). Fourth, the two sets of metaphors — "the starfish and the spider" and "the elephant and the rabbit" — reveal the resilience advantage of decentralized structures under persecution: every Oikos carries the complete DNA of the Kingdom, and is therefore "indestructible."
### Key Scriptures
• Genesis 18:19 — "For I have chosen him, that he may command his children and his household after him to keep the way of the Lord" (Oikos as the smallest operational unit for the transmission of faith)
• Deuteronomy 6:6-7 — "and talk of them when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise" (the round-the-clock prototype of family discipleship)
• 2 Timothy 1:5 — "a faith that dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice" (a New Testament example of the intergenerational discipleship chain)

## Chapter 5: The Ecology of the Kingdom Community — Koinonia and the Integrated Function of Society
### Chapter Overview
This chapter argues that Koinonia (the sharing of life) is far more than a "fellowship activity"; it is the core mechanism by which God establishes a complete Kingdom community on earth. The author first traces the integral blueprint of Old Testament Israel as a "theocratic society": the law covered justice (public judgment at the city gate), economics (the Jubilee system, the prohibition on lending at interest), social welfare (the gleaning laws) and education (the family transmission of Deuteronomy 6) — "these are a shadow of the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ." The Koinonia of the New Testament goes beyond the external constraints of the Old Testament law, relying on the indwelling of the Holy Spirit to realize shared life at a higher standard: from Acts 4:34, "there was not a needy person among them" (the promise of Deuteronomy 15:4 at last substantively fulfilled), to the cross-regional macro-ecological connection manifested in Paul's collection, to the witnessing power of the "Christian alternative community" that the Emperor Julian was compelled to acknowledge. With the dual structure of "micro inner circulation (the sharing of life within the Oikos) + macro outer circulation (the circulation of resources within the spiritual ecological network)," this chapter depicts how Koinonia builds a genuine "alternative society."
### Key Scriptures

## Chapter 11: The Loss of Discipleship—The Link Omitted by Systematic Theology
### Chapter Overview
This chapter diagnoses a theological blind spot that is widely overlooked: discipleship finds no independent place in the catalog of traditional systematic theology, but is fragmented and scattered into the doctrines of sanctification, ecclesiology, and missiology—with the result that "how one life grows out of another life" has never been pursued as an independent proposition. The author unfolds the diagnosis on four levels: in **content**, catechetical education stops at "biblical literacy," able to produce "believers with knowledge" yet struggling to produce "fathers with wisdom"—systematic theology has a well-developed doctrine of sanctification but no "doctrine of maturity"; in **form**, the one-to-many monologue from the platform structurally excludes "dialogue within context," and it is the latter (as shown in the three cases of Jesus training the twelve disciples) that is the primary means of cultivating spiritual discernment; in **goal**, the hidden logic of the church's evaluation system is "retaining people," which erodes the ontological logic of biblical discipleship to "send people out"; in **standard**, "discernment," which should occupy the center, is quietly displaced by the counterfeit of "obedience." The fourfold diagnosis points to one conclusion: the defect of traditional discipleship is not that it does something wrong, but that it has never been pursued as an independent theological proposition.

• Hebrews 5:12-14 — "For though by this time you ought to be teachers... those who have their powers of discernment trained by constant practice to distinguish good from evil"

### Theme One: The Ceiling of Content and Form

## Chapter Twelve: The Principles of Discipleship—Returning to the Biblical Prototype
### Chapter Overview
This chapter draws the classic principles of discipleship out of Scripture. Taking Deuteronomy 6 as its prototype (the exegesis of that prototype was completed in chapter four; this chapter only draws out its operational implications), it sums up four principles: the discipler's own life comes before the content of discipleship; teaching is "ground into" (*shanan*) rather than finished the moment it has been "talked through"; four life scenarios (sitting, walking, lying down, rising up) make all of life the stage for discipleship; and the father—not the religious expert—is the first executor. The chapter then anchors these principles in the practice of Jesus (being with precedes sending out, questions take the place of answers, immersive shared life) and of Paul (the "unity of teaching and example" witnessed in 2 Tim 3:10-11, in which *didaskalia* and *typos* are each indispensable). ## Chapter Thirteen: The Goal of Discipleship—Discernment and Spiritual Reproduction
### Chapter Overview
This chapter answers the origin question of discipleship: what, precisely, is it meant to produce? The author proposes two inseparable core goals. The first is inward-facing **discernment**—not the sum of knowledge, but the practical wisdom (*phronesis*) that discerns God's will in complex, competing situations; it can only be gained through long-term "practice" in actual situations, and through "careful weighing" of all teaching (1 Cor 14:29—where Paul, in the imperative, requires every listener to make an independent judgment) it becomes a cell of the church's corporate immune system; it is thus a thorough negation of "obedience equals maturity." The second is outward-facing **capacity for spiritual reproduction**—the "be fruitful and multiply" of the creation mandate and the "make disciples of all nations" of the redemption mandate are two promulgations of the same law of life; a mature disciple can not only discern, but can also transmit faith by example, moving from "being born" to "being able to give birth" (the four-generation transmission of 2 Tim 2:2), and is trained in the four areas of service, building up, expansion, and governance, prepared to be sent out to establish a new Oikos. The two goals are completely unified in purpose: discernment protects the purity of reproduction (the immune system), and reproduction verifies the maturity of discernment. Every Oikos is, in essence, both a training ground for discernment and a launchpad for reproduction.

## Chapter Fifteen: The Sacred Feast: The Original Face of the Lord's Supper and the Theology of the Table

## Chapter 16: Interactive Gathering: Monologue or Symphony?
### Chapter Overview
This chapter is the third step in the book's "Practice and Life" section, and the most hands-on chapter of all. The author argues that if the space of the gathering has already returned from the theater to the living room (Chapter 14), and the center of the gathering has already been restored from the sermon to the love feast (Chapter 15), yet the one-way pattern of "one person speaks, everyone listens in silence" is still maintained, then one has only opened a "miniature church" in the home—without ever touching the dynamic core of the Oikos. This chapter takes 1 Corinthians 14:26 as the "charter of the gathering" and argues from three angles that the interactive gathering is the normative pattern of the New Testament: (1) the Hekastos ("each one") of "each one has" establishes the right of every member to participate; (2) the Greek word dialegomai (dialogue) was the default mode of teaching in the apostolic era, not one-way lecturing; (3) tested against the three-tier criteria of Chapter 2, all three levels converge on the same conclusion. Women participate freely around the round table of "each one has," but do not take up the authoritative teaching role of the elders; as spiritual mothers they build up the Body within a broad space of service.

## Conclusion: A Thorough Return to Sola Scriptura

## Appendix: House Church Launch Roadmap
