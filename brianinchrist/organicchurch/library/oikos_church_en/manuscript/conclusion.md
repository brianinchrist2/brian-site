# Conclusion: The Radical Return to Sola Scriptura

## Foreword: A Candid Word

Before we enter this conclusion, I want to do something perhaps uncommon—to set aside the argument for a moment and speak a few words from the heart.

Across the journey of these eighteen chapters, I have led the reader along a long road: from diagnosing the structural predicament of the institutional church, to rediscovering the biblical foundations of the three pillars—Oikos, Koinonia, and Oikonomia—to sketching the practical blueprint of the house church in governance, gathering, and networks. I know that along the way, some of the book's phrasing may have been sharp, and some diagnoses may have touched the tender places of many faithful pastors.

I must candidly admit: these diagnoses are not verdicts pronounced from on high, but sighs uttered from within. My own spiritual life grew up in the soil of the institutional church, and many of the most devout, most sacrificial, most life-filled brothers and sisters I know remain quietly committed within the institutional church to this day. Toward them I carry deep respect from the bottom of my heart. The object of this book's critique has never been people—not the preachers who labor exhaustively from the pulpit, not the deacons who silently arrange the chairs on Sunday mornings, not the lay believers who give away the greater part of their salaries. What this book seeks to diagnose is structure, is system—those patterns that have drifted from the "pattern shown on the mountain" yet have become so familiar to us that we no longer notice them.

If you are a pastor serving in an institutional church, reading this book, and feeling unsettled as you read—please understand that unease as a sincere reminder between fellow workers, not an accusation from on high. This book's invitation is this: let us open the Scriptures together, let us return together to the presence of that "pattern shown on the mountain." Whatever the final conclusion may be—whether or not it agrees entirely with this book—this common return is itself an act of obedience to the principle of Sola Scriptura.

With this disposition in mind, let us enter this final review and prospect.

## I. Affirmation and Reservation: A Theological Assessment of the Institutional Church

Before issuing the final call, it is necessary to respond directly to the strongest objection this book is likely to face. Many faithful pastors and theologians will ask: since this book levels such a profound critique against the institutional church, does it thereby negate the legitimacy of all institutional churches across nearly two thousand years of Christian history?

The answer to this question must be carefully held between affirmation and distinction.

First, we must fairly acknowledge the enormous historical contribution of the institutional church. It was the institutional church that preserved and transmitted the canon of Scripture—through the long darkness of the Middle Ages, it was the monks in the monasteries, stroke by stroke copying the parchment scrolls, who kept the Word of God from being lost in the currents of history. Great spiritual revivals such as the Reformation, the Great Awakening, and the global missionary movement all occurred through institutional channels. Many of the greatest theologians, missionaries, and martyrs—from Augustine to Luther, from Wesley to Hudson Taylor—came from the traditions of the institutional church. In eras and regions where no house church network existed, the institutional church genuinely bore the historical mission of spreading the gospel and shepherding believers. The witnessing function of the church as an "alternative community" in the world, emphasized by John Stott in *Christian Mission in the Modern World*, has in history indeed been realized many times through the channels of the institutional church. These are facts of history—worthy of respect and to be gratefully remembered.

Yet, after this gratitude, we must introduce a crucial theological distinction—a core principle running throughout this book: God's **Sovereign Use** is not the same as His **Design Endorsement**.

We have already used the metaphor of "Balaam's donkey" for this distinction in the preface. God did indeed use Balaam's donkey to speak to the prophet (Num 22:28), but that does not mean the "donkey" is the standard pattern of the prophetic office. God used Nebuchadnezzar to accomplish His discipline of Israel, but the Babylonian Empire is certainly no model of God's kingdom. By the same logic, the fact that God has been able—and indeed has—done great things through the institutional church in history testifies to God's power and faithfulness, not to the institutional church as God's final revelation of the church's form. Once this distinction is ignored, any reflection on the form of the church is instantly equated with "negating everything," and genuine theological dialogue suffocates in that either-or logic.

The focus of this book's critique has never been wholesale negation, but a clear-eyed diagnosis of a specific distortion. When the maintenance of the institution itself becomes the primary goal, the church imperceptibly degenerates from a "sent missionary community" into a "self-perpetuating religious organization." When the cost of maintenance—property rent, paid staff, administrative expenses—overwhelms the cost of mission, structure itself becomes a stumbling block to mission. When professionalization reduces the laity to passivity, when "ministry" becomes a performance by a few on the stage and spectatorship by the many in the pews, the function of the Body of Christ is emasculated. When property assets become tied to ecclesial status, when "bigger and stronger" becomes a self-evident pursuit, the "temple complex" quietly takes root—and it is precisely this complex that Chapters 1 and 14 of this book diagnose as the core ailment.

In fact, history itself corroborates this diagnosis. Every true spiritual revival has been accompanied by a certain deconstruction of existing institutions—the Reformation broke the monopoly of Roman Catholicism, the rise of Methodism broke through the rigidity of the Church of England, and the explosive growth of the global South church has largely occurred in its least institutionalized, most "informal" stage. In *The Problem of Wineskins*, Howard Snyder demonstrates with a wealth of historical cases a thought-provoking pattern: the vitality of the church and its degree of institutionalization tend to stand in inverse proportion—not because "organization" is wrong in itself, but because when the logic of the organization begins to override the logic of life, the wineskin can no longer hold the new wine.

This is not arrogance toward history; it is history itself crying out.

So, facing the present, our position can be stated thus: if your institutional church is still mission-centered, still willing to judge its own traditions by Scripture, still sincerely seeking how to be more faithful to the "pattern shown on the mountain," then this book is not urging you to "leave," but inviting you to begin reflection and renewal within your present situation. But if you find that the institution you belong to has become a "maintenance machine"—whose main energy goes to preserving its own operations, whose main resources go to sustaining its own structures, whose main metrics are attendance figures and offering amounts—then the house church model may indeed be an invitation from the Spirit to you, inviting you back to a faith practice closer to the original pattern of the New Testament church.

Our position is neither to absolutize the house church nor to negate the institutional church, but to make a Scripture-based judgment between the two: in the present age, the house church model represents a form of the church closer to the New Testament revelation, and it deserves to be taken seriously, studied deeply, and practiced courageously.

## II. Not Innovation but Obedience: Returning to the "Pattern Shown on the Mountain"

At this point in the argument, we must return to a fundamental clarification running through the whole book: this "ontological revolution" of the house church is, in essence, not an "innovation" seeking novelty, but a thorough "return to the sources" (*Ad Fontes*).

Just as Martin Luther and Calvin in the Reformation called the church back to the doctrinal foundation of Sola Scriptura, so today what we are calling for is that Sola Scriptura be extended from soteriology to ecclesiology—not only letting Scripture tell us "how to be saved," but also letting Scripture tell us "how to do church." For a long time, many evangelical churches have firmly held Sola Scriptura in soteriology, yet in ecclesiology have unconsciously embraced pragmatism and cultural tradition. We built a vast religious machine from the administrative architecture of the Roman Empire (bureaucracy), the rhetorical tradition of Greece (one-way preaching), and the medieval sacramental view (the Eucharist as ritual)—while forgetting that unalterable sacred blueprint promulgated on Sinai and Zion: "See that you make them after the pattern for them, which is being shown you on the mountain" (Ex 25:40; Heb 8:5).

The house church movement is a renewed obedience to this ancient command. It declares: God is not only Lord of redemption, but also Lord of the church's form. He who designed life also designed the vessel in which life grows. We have no right to amend "the apostolic pattern" with "modern efficiency."

Here I want to make a methodological return that runs through the whole book. This claim about the normativity of the church's form does not rest on a single text, but simultaneously satisfies the three-level criterion established in Chapter 2 of this book. At the level of explicit teaching, Exodus 25:40 and Hebrews 8:5 directly command us to build "after the pattern shown on the mountain"—this is the direct revelation of God's concern for the form of the church. At the level of repetition, from the Acts of the Apostles to the Pauline epistles, house gatherings (rather than gatherings in public buildings) are the pervasive, repeated pattern of the New Testament church—Acts 2:46, 5:42, 20:20; Rom 16:5; 1 Cor 16:19; Col 4:15; Phlm 1:2. Such repetition is not coincidence; it points to a norm. At the level of the trajectory of redemptive history, the pattern of God's presence—from tabernacle to temple to the incarnation of Christ to His dwelling in the hearts of believers—traces a clear trajectory from concentration toward dispersion, from buildings toward life. At this core proposition, the three levels converge on the same conclusion: the form of the church is not an insignificant technical matter; it concerns God's design intent for the church at the ontological level.

Therefore, this is not merely a physical relocation of the gathering from a church building to a living room; it is a battle over sovereignty: who defines the church? Human religious tradition, or the eternal Word of God? To return to Oikos is to acknowledge the absolute authority of God as the great Architect. We do this not because we think lightly of the witness of the institutional churches of past generations, but precisely because we believe that a faithful return to the "pattern shown on the mountain" is the deepest honor we can pay to those who came before us—was not the very thing they spent their lives pursuing a church more faithful to the Word of God?

## III. The Reconstruction of Oikos, Koinonia, and Oikonomia: Answering Jesus' Prayer

In this book we have devoted great length to rediscovering the three pillars: Oikos (the household), Koinonia (life-sharing), and Oikonomia (stewardship). But at this moment of conclusion, I would remind the reader: these three are not ultimate ends; they are only vessels. Their sole reason for existence is to bear and fulfill that most profound, most painful, and most glorious prayer Jesus offered before He departed:

This "High Priestly Prayer" is the ballast and the ultimate destination of house church theology. All the argument of this book—from the diagnosis of Chapter 1 to the networks of Chapter 18—ultimately exists to answer one question: what form of the church can best bear the kind of unity this prayer depicts?

Let us re-examine each face of the sacred triangle, and see how each points to this ultimate unity.

**Oikos (the household)—the vessel of Trinitarian unity.** The unity Jesus prays for is not organizational merger, nor administrative unification, but a mutual indwelling of life "as the Father and the Son"—theologians use "Perichoresis" (mutual interpenetration, mutual indwelling) to describe this intimacy of "I in you and you in me" within the Trinity. This deep, organic unity is extremely difficult to realize in a large auditorium gathering of hundreds facing a platform—not because God's power is limited, but because this unity requires the relational density of face-to-face, mutually knowing intimacy as its vessel. Only in a covenant family like the Oikos—in the scene of a dozen people seated around the same table, opening their lives to one another—can the unity Jesus prayed for find its most natural foothold. Oikos is not a tool of efficiency; it is the holographic projection of the Triune God in human life.

**Koinonia (life-sharing)—the tangible witness of love.** Jesus said: "I have made known to them your name…so that the love with which you have loved me may be in them, and I in them" (John 17:26). Koinonia is not merely a warm feeling at a gathering; it is the embodied circulation of the love of the Triune God among believers. Through the shared table of the love feast, through mutual provision that abolishes lack, through the humble service of washing one another's feet, this originally abstract and invisible love is transformed into a witness that the world can see, can touch, and be drawn toward. This is exactly what Jesus meant by "that the world may believe"—the world does not believe because it hears a brilliant sermon, but because it sees a real love among a people that transcends blood and interest, and is shaken by it. As I argued in Chapter 5, the fundamental reason the Pentecostal church expanded so rapidly within a hostile Roman Empire was not its brilliant strategy, but that it fulfilled a promise Old Testament Israel never fulfilled—"there need be no poor people among you" (Deut 15:4; cf. Acts 4:34).

**Oikonomia (stewardship)—the transmission mechanism of glory.** Jesus prayed: "The glory that you have given me I have given to them, that they may be one even as we are one" (John 17:22). Notice the astonishing chain of logic: the Father gives glory to the Son, the Son gives glory to the church—glory is distributed *downward*, not concentrated *upward*. True Oikonomia is not control, not dominion, but distribution—just as the Father gives glory to the Son, and the Son to the church, so the elders and fathers of the house church should distribute authority and glory to every member, equipping them for their respective work (Eph 4:12), so that every child filled with the Spirit becomes a bearer of glory. This pattern of governance is fully consistent with the "fatherly governance" depicted in Chapters 8 and 9 of this book—a good father is not the one who always stands at center stage, but the one who keeps turning the spotlight toward his children, gladly retreating behind the scenes for their growth.

Therefore, the house church movement is not merely about "strategy"; in essence it is Christological and Trinitarian. It is the church striving to become the vessel of that "perfected unity," so that the Father's heart may be satisfied. Every small Oikos, every genuine Koinonia, every act of father-hearted Oikonomia is a response to Jesus' prayer that has not yet been fully answered—"that they may be one."

## IV. The Dialectic of History and the Stewardship of Generations

Before looking to the future, we need to examine this movement from a broader historical perspective and establish our own generation's place within it.

The long river of history shows us a profound dialectical law: the rise of the new does not mean the complete extinction of the old, but the appearance of the new often becomes the catalyst for the old's renewal. The rise of Christianity did not make Judaism disappear; the rise of Protestantism in the Reformation did not drive Catholicism entirely off the historical stage—but the impact of Protestantism objectively compelled the Catholic Church to undertake profound internal reflection and renewal (the Council of Trent is clear evidence).

Likewise, the rediscovery and rise of the house church will not, and should not, completely replace the historical existence of the institutional church. They may coexist for a considerable period. But we are convinced that the rise of the house church movement will become a thorn with love—it will, to a greater or lesser degree, provoke the traditional institutional church to reflect on its ponderous structures, to question the traditions it takes for granted, and ultimately to move toward change in a Scriptural direction.

Lesslie Newbigin made a very far-sighted observation in *The Household of God*. He pointed out that the unity of the church is not achieved through administrative unification, nor maintained by eliminating differences, but demonstrated through common obedience to the mission of God. Therefore, the most meaningful dialogue between the house church and the institutional church is not to argue over "who is the true church," but to jointly ask "whether we are all faithfully executing the mission of God." This book was written out of precisely such a question.

At the same time, we must also maintain a clear historical humility. Advancing this ontological revolution of the house church is by no means an easy road. Before us, many pioneers have already paid the price. We know well that we have chosen a "narrow gate." But the driving force behind this choice is by no means a reactive response to external environmental pressure, nor a desire to be novel, but reverence for the sacred revelation of Scripture and absolute loyalty to Jesus Christ. This is entirely a vision-led practice of faith, not merely an occasion-driven response to circumstances.

We need a clear generational positioning. What we are doing now may still be preparatory work for this grand renewal. Just as David, though unable to build the temple himself, spent his life preparing abundant gold, silver, bronze, iron, and timber for Solomon (1 Chron 22:14), so we are the generation that prepares the materials—not builders who are prominent before people, but faithful road-pavers in the shadows. This may take several generations to complete. We may not see all the fruit of this movement within our own lifetimes, but that does not diminish the meaning of acting now.

In the end, what evaluates our work is not the success-and-failure judgment of the age, but the word of Jesus Christ: "Well done, good and faithful servant" (Matt 25:21).

## V. The Call: Come Out of Babylon, Enter the New Wineskins

Dear fellow workers and brothers and sisters, let us return to the starting point of this book.

From Chapter 1 to Chapter 18, from diagnosis to construction, from theology to practice, from the microscopic Oikos to the macroscopic apostolic network, this book has been doing only one thing: attempting to answer a simple and fundamental question—according to the revelation of Scripture, what should the church be like?

I believe the answer of Scripture is clear enough. The church is a living organism whose substance is the covenant household—not a religious institution centered on a building. Its vessel is Oikos, its bloodstream is Koinonia, its nervous system is Oikonomia. It is like a cell rather than a mansion, like a starfish rather than a spider, like a fueling station rather than a terminal. It is simple, organic, self-multiplying, decentralized, wild and untamable—and yet it is precisely this thing that appears so small and so fragile that has again and again in history proved its astonishing vitality and penetrating power.

The old wineskin of the institutional church, in many places, can no longer bear the new wine the Spirit is pouring out in this new age. Grand buildings, costly maintenance, a professionalized clergy—are becoming in more and more contexts a burden to the expansion of the Kingdom rather than a help. When God shakes heaven and earth, only those things that cannot be shaken (Heb 12:27) will remain.

God is calling His people out of the bondage of formalism, consumerism, and the sacred-secular dichotomy. This is not merely an adjustment of the form of gathering; it is a spiritual exodus.

This call demands courage.

It demands that we have the courage to tear down the idol of the "temple complex" in our hearts—to stop measuring the "success" of the church by grand architecture and bustling spectacle. We must learn to see greatness in the small, and glory in the humble. Where two or three are gathered in the name of the Lord (Matt 18:20), there is the Holy of Holies. An ordinary living room, a table spread with simple food, a company of brothers and sisters willing to open their lives to one another—this is the church, the complete church, the church without discount.

It demands that we have the courage to let go—to stop clinging to dependence on "professional speakers" and "celebrity pastors," and to trust the Spirit's movement in every humble member. We must dare to let gatherings become "unpredictable," to let gifts flow freely, to let every voice have a chance to be heard. God can speak through the mouth of a donkey (Num 22:28); how much more can He speak through every child filled with the Spirit.

It demands that we have the courage to open up—to open our closed doors, to set a simple table, to receive the wounded world. The true gospel is not only proclaimed from the pulpit; it is handed across tables and lived out in daily life. When an unbeliever walks into your home, sits at your table, and sees a love between you and your brothers and sisters that transcends blood, what he encounters is not a "religion"—he encounters a living God.

Let us return to simplicity. Let us return to the organic. Let us return to that apostolic life that is wild, untamable, yet conquers the world.

The house church is not a reluctant fallback, but a secret weapon in the hand of God—that unseen seed, that tiny mustard seed, that leaven which at last causes the whole lump to rise (Matt 13:33). In this turbulent age, may we, sojourners of the end times, no longer linger over the fleshpots of Egypt, nor put our faith in the high towers of Babylon. May we, in countless small living rooms, in ordinary breaking of bread and conversation, witness the real presence of the risen Lord. May our homes become ladders reaching to heaven, may our tables become the feast of the Kingdom, may our lives become living sacrifices (Rom 12:1).

Until that day, when the trumpet sounds and the New Jerusalem descends from heaven, we shall see that city with no temple—for its temple is the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb (Rev 21:22). Then we shall understand that every small Oikos we have had on earth is but a shadow and foretaste of that eternal city. Every breaking of bread in our living rooms today is a rehearsal for the feast of the Kingdom; every life-sharing around our tables today is a rehearsal for that ultimate unity.

Maranatha! Come, Lord Jesus!
